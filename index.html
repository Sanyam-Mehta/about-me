Point Prompting: Counterfactual Tracking with Video Diffusion Models

Sanyam Mehta*, Ayush Shrivastava*, Daniel Geng, Andrew Owens
ICLR 2026
project page / arXiv / ICLR

Do video diffusion models, which make videos by cleaning up noise step by step, already know how points move? We put a colored marker on a point in the first frame, had an off-the-shelf model regenerate the video, and followed the marker. This gives us tracking with no training.

More

We also gave the model the unedited first frame as a negative prompt, an example of what not to produce. That keeps the marker from fading away.

The tracking works with different kinds of video models and holds up when the point is hidden for a while. It beats earlier trackers that also skip training, and is competitive with self-supervised trackers, which were trained for the job on unlabeled video.

* equal contribution

JEVA: Joint Encoders for Variational Action World Models

Aditya Singh, Sanyam Mehta, Yixin Wang, Nima Fazeli, Bernadette Bucher
Under review, 2026
project page

Robot action labels are scarce, but video is plentiful. JEVA learns a world model, a model that predicts what happens next, and learns its actions from video instead of being given them. With 5% of episodes labeled, the policy it learns (the part that picks actions) matches or beats baselines trained with 20% on the OGBench cube-stacking tasks.

More

These learned actions are called latent actions. JEVA has two parts. An inverse dynamics model guesses the action that happened between two frames, and a forward model predicts the next state. JEVA trains both together in one stage, from mostly unlabeled video plus a few labeled episodes.

I tested a discrete codebook for the latent actions, a fixed set of action codes to pick from, and continuous latents worked better. I also helped compare two planners, MPPI and CEM, in the learned latent space. CEM won consistently, and that started the project below.

Planning in latent-action world models

Independent project, Oct 2026 to present

CEM and MPPI are two common sampling planners. In JEVA, CEM kept beating MPPI, and I wanted to know if that meant the learned action space was badly behaved, so that averaging good actions gives a bad one. On LeWM, a public model, a 2×2 experiment showed most of the gap came from how each planner handles its sampling noise, not from the space itself. Next I'm testing the original idea directly.

More

Each round, both planners try a few hundred candidate action sequences in the world model, then move toward the good ones. CEM averages its best few and shrinks its sampling noise as it goes. MPPI takes a score-weighted average of many and keeps its noise fixed.

With LeWM on Push-T, a task where the robot pushes a T-shaped block to a goal position and angle, CEM led by over 20 points with the same compute. The two planners differ in two ways at once, so I swapped each difference on its own. Letting MPPI shrink its noise as it goes took it from 66% to 84%. So the gap no longer counts as evidence that the space is irregular.

I then ran follow-up runs with my predictions written down beforehand. I predicted that MPPI's weighting with CEM's noise rule would reach at least 84%. It reached 82%, so I'm not reading anything into the weighting yet.

For the direct test, I'll take CEM's best action samples, run each one, then run their average, and see whether the averages do systematically worse. If they don't, I'll write it up as a negative result.

Robot-agnostic navigation from video models

University of Michigan, Jul 2025 to Jan 2026, advised by Bernadette Bucher

Can a robot follow a language instruction, like "go past the kitchen and stop at the stairs," by having a video model imagine the walk? I built the first stage, which picks the view facing the goal. On scenes it had never seen, it was within 15° of the right direction 76% of the time, against 12.5% by chance.

More

I started with a diffusion policy, a model that generates actions by cleaning up noise step by step. It predicted waypoints from the instruction and the current view of a feature map of the scene that stores what each spot looks like. Its real flaw was that it only saw the present. It had no memory of where it had been, and following an instruction depends on that history.

Video models already use past frames and text, so I changed plans. Pick the camera view that faces the goal. Generate a video from that view and the instruction. Then recover the camera motion by matching frames. Navigation turns into pose estimation, working out how the camera moved, and any robot can follow that. It also gets better as video models get better. It's the same idea as Point Prompting, reading motion out of what a video model generates.

The first stage looks at 24 views around the robot, 15° apart. I trained it on about 10.7K episodes I collected myself in the Habitat simulator. I finished my degree before building the later stages.
