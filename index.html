Point Prompting: Counterfactual Tracking with Video Diffusion Models

Sanyam Mehta*, Ayush Shrivastava*, Daniel Geng, Andrew Owens
ICLR 2026
project page / arXiv / ICLR

Do video diffusion models already know how points move? We put a colored marker on a point in the first frame, had an off-the-shelf model regenerate the video, and followed the marker. Tracking falls out of this with no training, and it is competitive with self-supervised trackers that were trained for the job.

More

Video diffusion models make videos by starting from noise and cleaning it up step by step. We wanted to know if they already understand how points move. So we put a colored marker on a point in the first frame, had an off-the-shelf model regenerate the video, and followed the marker. We also gave it the unedited video as a negative prompt, an example of what not to produce, which keeps the marker from fading away.

Tracking falls out of this with no training at all. It works with different kinds of video models, holds up when the point is hidden for a while, beats earlier trackers that also skip training, and is competitive with self-supervised trackers, which were trained for the job on unlabeled video.

JEVA: Joint Encoders for Variational Action World Models

Aditya Singh, Sanyam Mehta, Yixin Wang, Nima Fazeli, Bernadette Bucher
Under review, 2026
project page

Robot action labels are scarce, but video is plentiful. JEVA learns a world model, a model that predicts what happens next, with actions learned from mostly unlabeled video. With 5% of episodes labeled, its policy matches or beats baselines trained with 20%.

More

Robots need action labels to learn from video, and those labels are scarce. Video itself is everywhere. JEVA learns a world model, a model that predicts what happens next, whose actions are latent. That means the actions are learned from video instead of given.

It has two parts. An inverse dynamics model guesses the action that happened between two frames, and a forward model predicts the next state. JEVA trains both together in one stage, from mostly unlabeled video plus a few labeled episodes. With only 5% of episodes labeled, the policy it learns, the part that picks the robot's actions, matches or beats baselines trained with 20%.

I tested a discrete codebook for the latent actions, a fixed set of action codes to pick from, and continuous latents worked better. I also helped compare two planners, MPPI and CEM, in the learned latent space. CEM won consistently, and that started the project below.

Planning in latent-action world models

Independent project, Oct 2026 to present

In JEVA, CEM kept beating MPPI, two common sampling planners. I wanted to know if that meant the learned action space was badly behaved. A 2×2 experiment showed most of the gap came from how each planner handles its sampling noise, not from the space itself. Next I'm testing the original idea directly.

More

CEM and MPPI are both sampling planners. Each round they try a few hundred candidate action sequences in the world model, then move toward the good ones. CEM averages its best few and shrinks its sampling noise as it goes. MPPI takes a score-weighted average of many and keeps its noise fixed.

In JEVA, CEM kept beating MPPI. I saw the same thing on LeWM, a public latent-action world model, on Push-T, a task where the robot pushes a T-shaped block to a goal position and angle. With the same compute, CEM led by 23 points. My question was whether this meant the learned action space is badly behaved, so that averaging good actions lands somewhere the model doesn't understand.

The two planners differ in two ways at once, so I swapped each difference on its own in a 2×2 experiment. Giving MPPI CEM's shrinking noise took it from 66% to 84%. Most of the gap came from the noise schedule, not the latent space, so the gap no longer counts as evidence that the space is irregular. I then ran follow-up runs with my predictions written down beforehand. One of them narrowly failed.

Next I want to test the original idea directly. I'll take CEM's best action samples, run each one, then run their average, and see whether the averages do systematically worse. If they don't, I'll write it up as a negative result.

Robot-agnostic navigation from video models

University of Michigan, Jul 2025 to Jan 2026, advised by Bernadette Bucher

Can a robot follow a language instruction by letting a video model imagine the walk, then working out how the camera moved in those imagined frames? That turns navigation into something any robot can follow. I built the first stage, which picks the view facing the goal. On unseen scenes it was within 15° of the right direction 76% of the time, against 12.5% by chance.

More

The goal was a robot that follows a language instruction, like "go past the kitchen and stop at the stairs." I started with a diffusion policy, a model that generates actions by cleaning up noise step by step. It predicted waypoints from the instruction and the current view of a semantic map, a map labeled with what is where. Its real flaw was that it only saw the present. It had no memory of where it had been, and following an instruction depends on that history.

Video models condition on past frames and text by construction, and that led to a new plan. Pick the camera view that faces the goal. Have a video generation model imagine the walk from that view and the instruction. Then recover the camera motion by matching frames. Navigation turns into pose estimation, working out how the camera moved, and any robot can follow that. It also gets better as video models get better, and it ties back to Point Prompting.

I built the first stage. It looks at 24 views around the robot, 15° apart, and picks the one leading toward the goal. I trained it on about 10.7K episodes I collected myself in the Habitat simulator. On scenes it had never seen, it was within 15° of the right direction 76% of the time, against 12.5% by chance. I finished my degree before building the later stages.
